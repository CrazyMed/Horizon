## [Developer Argues AI Won't Speed Up Processes Due to Unclear Requirements](https://frederickvanbrabant.com/blog/2026-05-15-i-dont-think-ai-will-make-your-processes-go-faster/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

A software developer argues that AI tools like LLMs won't actually accelerate software development processes, because poorly-defined requirements and vague feature requests have always been the real bottleneck—and AI forces teams to articulate these issues more clearly rather than bypassing them. This challenges the prevailing narrative that AI will dramatically boost developer productivity. If the bottleneck isn't coding but requirements clarity, organizations may need to rethink their AI adoption strategies and invest more in requirements engineering rather than expecting AI to magically solve slowdowns. The article notes that development teams frequently receive vague requirements like 'Get data and give it to the user' with no specification of data sources, storage, or return formats. While some commenters disagree, arguing AI can speed up ideation, documentation, and deployment, the author contends that AI can't shortcut the fundamental need for precise problem definitions.

**Background**: Requirements engineering is a critical phase in software development that involves gathering, analyzing, and specifying what a system should do. Studies show that unclear or incomplete requirements are a leading cause of project failure and cost overruns. Large language models (LLMs) have been widely adopted for code generation and development assistance since 2023, but their impact on the upstream requirements phase remains limited since they can only work with the inputs they receive.

## [GDS Issues 'Open by Default' Guidance After NHS Closes Repositories](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/17/gds-weighs-in/#atom-everything) ⭐️ 6.0/10

The UK Government Digital Service published guidance titled "AI, open code and vulnerability risk in the public sector" on May 14, 2026, recommending that public sector code remain "open by default" rather than being made private in response to vulnerability disclosures. This guidance appears to be a direct response to the NHS's controversial decision to close its open source repositories following vulnerability reports from Anthropic's Project Glasswing. This represents a rare public intervention by GDS in what is typically an internal civil service matter, signaling that the government's central digital authority disagrees with the NHS's approach to open source security. The guidance could set a precedent for how public sector organizations balance transparency with security concerns, potentially influencing technology policy across UK government agencies. GDS explicitly stated that "making everything private adds additional delivery and policy costs, and can reduce reuse and scrutiny," recommending that closure be "used sparingly and deliberately." The NHS decision has been described as "poorly considered" by commentators, and Terence Eden interprets the GDS guidance as a significant escalation—comparing it to the civil service expression of being "invited to a meeting without biscuits," implying an unusually frosty public disagreement.

**Background**: Project Glasswing is Anthropic's coordinated vulnerability disclosure initiative that uses their Claude Mythos Preview AI model to identify security issues in critical infrastructure, partnering with organizations responsible for essential services. The Government Digital Service is the central unit within the UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology responsible for transforming online public services and setting digital standards across government. Within UK civil service culture, being invited to a "meeting without biscuits" is a well-known expression denoting a particularly stern or uncomfortable discussion where normal courtesies are absent.
