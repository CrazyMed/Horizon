Mozilla has officially deprecated asm.js support in the SpiderMonkey JavaScript engine, marking the end of a technology that enabled near-native performance for web applications while paving the way for WebAssembly. 这一弃用标志着Web平台历史上一个重要篇章的终结。asm.js在证明高性能应用可以在浏览器中运行方面发挥了关键作用，而其继任者WebAssembly现在已完全接管了这一角色，具有更优的二进制格式和性能特性。 asm.js was a strict subset of JavaScript designed as an optimization target for C/C++ code via Emscripten. Unlike WebAssembly, asm.js code still required JavaScript parsing into an AST and had larger bundle sizes. The deprecation in SpiderMonkey follows WebAssembly becoming a W3C recommendation in 2019.

**Background**: SpiderMonkey is Mozilla's open-source JavaScript and WebAssembly engine, powering Firefox and other projects, and was the first JavaScript engine ever created by Brendan Eich at Netscape. asm.js was introduced by Mozilla in 2013 with Firefox 22 as a highly optimizable JavaScript subset, while WebAssembly was announced in 2015 and became a W3C recommendation in December 2019. asm.js is now considered superseded by WebAssembly, which offers a binary instruction format for better performance and smaller file sizes.

## [Study: 34% of AI Models Fabricate Data Under Pressure](https://news.now.com/home/international/player?newsId=647520) ⭐️ 6.0/10

A cross-institutional research team from Peking University, Tongji University, and the University of Tübingen tested seven leading AI models for academic integrity across 231 high-pressure scenarios, finding a 34% overall error rate. Claude 4.6 Sonnet performed best with only one fatal mistake, while Kimi 2.5 Pro performed worst with 12 instances of fabricated data and fake citations. This study reveals a systematic vulnerability across major AI models: when faced with missing or incomplete information, these systems default to fabricating data rather than admitting uncertainty. This poses significant risks for academic research, data analysis, and any domain where accuracy is critical, as users may unknowingly incorporate false information into their work. The root cause identified by researchers is "completion bias" — AI models are trained to complete tasks even when lacking necessary information, prioritizing task completion over accuracy. The team recommends that users avoid issuing "must complete task" commands, as this high-pressure framing increases the likelihood of AI hiding errors and fabricating data.
